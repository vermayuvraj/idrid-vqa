\begin{abstract}
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of preventable blindness, and
automated grading is widely proposed as a means of extending screening capacity.
Most deployed systems, however, are opaque classifiers that emit a severity label
without justification, while the vision-language models (VLMs) capable of
articulating their reasoning are generally assumed to require data-centre
accelerators for adaptation. This paper examines whether clinically useful,
\emph{explainable} DR grading can instead be obtained on a single consumer GPU.
We construct \textbf{IDRiD-VQA}, a visual-question-answering corpus of 2{,}064
label-grounded question--answer pairs spanning grade identification, lesion
identification, clinical reasoning and patient recommendation, derived from the
516 fundus photographs of the IDRiD disease-grading subset. We then adapt
Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct using 4-bit quantised low-rank adaptation, training only
28.9M parameters (1.45\% of the model) within a 6\,GB memory envelope. On the
canonical 103-image test split, adaptation raises exact-match grading accuracy
from 31.1\% to 66.0\% and quadratic weighted kappa from 0.00 to 0.76, while
roughly tripling ROUGE-L agreement of the generated explanations and reducing
inference latency by 47\%. A controlled ablation isolates \emph{input resolution}
as the dominant factor: increasing the shorter training resolution from 512 to
768 pixels contributes 11.6 accuracy points and 0.14 kappa at negligible memory
cost, because aggressive downsampling destroys the microaneurysms that define
early disease. The complete fine-tune requires approximately 90 minutes and peaks
at 3.52\,GB of VRAM. A per-class analysis, however, qualifies these aggregate
figures sharply: the adapted model collapses onto the two majority grades and
never predicts mild retinopathy, so accuracy and ordinal agreement conceal a
complete failure at the stage where screening is most actionable. We release the
dataset, adapter weights and code to support reproducible low-resource medical
VLM research, and we argue that aggregate agreement should not be mistaken for
clinical readiness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Diabetic retinopathy is a microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and one
of the principal causes of avoidable vision loss worldwide; recent
meta-analytic estimates place global prevalence among people with diabetes near
one in five, with the absolute burden projected to grow substantially through
2045~\cite{teo2021global}. Screening programmes rely on the interpretation of
colour fundus photographs against the International Clinical Diabetic
Retinopathy scale, which partitions severity into five ordinal
grades~\cite{wilkinson2003proposed}. The clinical bottleneck is not imaging but
interpretation: the supply of trained graders is far smaller than the population
requiring periodic examination.

Automated grading has therefore attracted sustained attention, and
convolutional systems trained on large retinal corpora now achieve
specialist-level discrimination for referable
disease~\cite{gulshan2016development,ting2017development}. These systems share a
structural limitation: they return a scalar severity estimate with no account of
the retinal findings that produced it. In a clinical workflow this is
consequential. A grader cannot audit a label, a trainee cannot learn from it, and
a regulator cannot inspect the basis of a recommendation. Post-hoc saliency
methods are frequently offered as a remedy, but their reliability as
explanations of medical model behaviour has been forcefully
questioned~\cite{ghassemi2021false}.

Vision-language models suggest a different route. A model that emits text can, in
principle, name the lesions it observes, connect them to a severity criterion, and
propose a follow-up interval---producing an artefact a clinician can read and
contest rather than a number they must trust. Instruction-tuned VLMs have been
adapted to biomedical domains with encouraging
results~\cite{li2023llavamed,liu2023llava}, yet the prevailing assumption is that
such adaptation demands multiple high-memory accelerators. That assumption has a
distributional consequence: it concentrates capability in well-funded
institutions and excludes precisely those low-resource settings where screening
shortfalls are most acute.

This paper tests the assumption directly. We ask whether a compact 2B-parameter
VLM can be adapted for explainable DR grading within the memory budget of a
consumer laptop GPU, and what such a system can and cannot deliver. Our
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{IDRiD-VQA}, a 2{,}064-pair visual-question-answering corpus for
explainable DR assessment, generated from expert labels rather than pixels so
that every reference answer is consistent with ground truth by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset}).
\item A memory-frugal adaptation recipe that fits training of
Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct into 3.52\,GB of VRAM and approximately 90 minutes on a
single 6\,GB GPU (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item An empirical finding of practical consequence: for this task
\emph{input resolution dominates} the conventional levers of epoch count and
optimiser schedule, contributing 11.6 accuracy points at essentially no memory
cost (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A critical appraisal of the resulting system, including an error analysis
showing that residual failures concentrate in proliferative disease, and an
argument that the label-conditioned construction of our corpus bounds what the
explanations can be claimed to demonstrate (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Automated Diabetic Retinopathy Grading}
Deep convolutional classifiers established the modern baseline for DR grading.
Gulshan \textit{et al.}~\cite{gulshan2016development} demonstrated
ophthalmologist-comparable sensitivity for referable disease on large clinical
corpora, and Ting \textit{et al.}~\cite{ting2017development} confirmed
generalisation across multiethnic screening populations. The IDRiD
dataset~\cite{porwal2018idrid} contributed pixel-level lesion annotation to a
subset of images and anchored a public grading challenge whose results remain the
standard reference point for this corpus~\cite{porwal2020challenge}. Subsequent
architectural work has exploited the clinical correlation between retinopathy and
macular oedema; CANet~\cite{li2019canet} introduced cross-disease attention and
reports the strongest joint grading accuracy on the IDRiD benchmark. All of these
systems are discriminative: they optimise an ordinal objective and return a
grade, not an account of it.

\subsection{Vision-Language Models in Medicine}
General-purpose instruction-tuned VLMs couple a vision encoder to a language
decoder and can be steered by natural-language prompts~\cite{liu2023llava}. The
Qwen2-VL family~\cite{wang2024qwen2vl} is notable for native dynamic resolution,
processing images at variable token budgets rather than a fixed grid---a property
that proves central to our results. Domain adaptation to biomedicine has been
demonstrated by LLaVA-Med~\cite{li2023llavamed}, which curates biomedical
instruction data to specialise a general assistant. Reviews of the field
emphasise that the promise of such models in medical imaging remains contingent
on rigorous evaluation rather than fluency alone~\cite{rajpurkar2022ai}.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora} freezes pretrained weights and
learns low-rank update matrices, reducing trainable parameters by orders of
magnitude. QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} composes this with 4-bit NormalFloat
quantisation of the frozen backbone, making single-GPU adaptation of large models
feasible. Kernel-level implementations such as Unsloth~\cite{unsloth2024} further
reduce activation memory through fused operations and custom gradient
checkpointing. Our work combines these components, but its contribution is not
the components themselves: it is the empirical demonstration that their
composition suffices for an explainable clinical task, and the identification of
resolution rather than adapter capacity as the binding constraint.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Source Corpus and Split Protocol}
We build on the IDRiD disease-grading subset~\cite{porwal2018idrid}, comprising
516 colour fundus photographs at $4288\times2848$ pixels, each annotated with an
ICDR severity grade $g \in \{0,\dots,4\}$ and a macular-oedema risk label. We
adopt the canonical challenge partition of 413 training and 103 testing
images~\cite{porwal2020challenge} to preserve comparability with prior work.

An implementation detail materially affects correctness: the training and testing
directories reuse filenames, so an image identifier such as
\texttt{IDRiD\_001} is ambiguous across splits. We therefore key every record by
the pair (split, identifier) rather than by identifier alone. Keying by filename
silently merges the partitions and inflates apparent performance; we report this
because the failure mode is easy to introduce and hard to detect downstream.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dist} shows the resulting grade distribution. The corpus is
markedly imbalanced: moderate (grade~2) and absent (grade~0) disease together
account for 65\% of training images, whereas mild disease (grade~1) is
represented by only 20 training and 5 testing images. This skew is a property of
the source data, not of our sampling, and it conditions the interpretation of
per-class results in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{grade_distribution.pdf}
\caption{ICDR grade distribution of the IDRiD disease-grading subset under the
canonical 413/103 partition. Mild disease (grade~1) is severely

\label{fig:dist}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Label-Grounded Question Answering}
Adapting a generative model requires supervision in natural language, which IDRiD
does not provide. We synthesise it. For each image we assemble a structured
record containing the severity grade, the macular-oedema risk and a vector of
lesion-presence indicators for microaneurysms, haemorrhages, hard exudates and
soft exudates. Where segmentation masks exist we derive indicators from non-zero
pixel support; for the grading-only images we fall back to grade-conditioned
clinical priors and record the provenance of every indicator so that inferred and
measured evidence remain distinguishable.

These structured records---and not the images---are then supplied to a Gemini
Flash model~\cite{sandler2024gemini}, which is prompted to produce four
question--answer pairs per case covering (i) grade identification, (ii) lesion
identification, (iii) clinical reasoning and (iv) patient recommendation.
Conditioning the generator on labels rather than pixels is a deliberate design
choice with a specific consequence: because the generator never observes the
fundus photograph, it cannot hallucinate findings absent from the ground truth,
and every reference answer is consistent with the expert annotation by
construction. The cost of this choice is a corresponding limit on explanation
diversity, which we address in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Generation is batched at eight cases per request and rate-limited; because
free-tier quotas are enforced per model, we cycle a pool of Gemini Flash variants
as each quota is exhausted, with a deterministic clinical template as a
guaranteed fallback. The procedure yields \textbf{2{,}064 pairs}
(4 per image over all 516 images), of which 1{,}652 form the training set and 412
the held-out set. In the run reported here every pair was model-generated; no
template fallback was required.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Backbone and Adaptation}
We adapt Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct~\cite{wang2024qwen2vl}, whose frozen weights are
loaded in 4-bit NormalFloat precision following QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}.
For a frozen projection $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, adaptation learns a
low-rank correction realised as the product of two thin matrices,

\begin{equation}
h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x,
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}

with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ and
$r \ll \min(d,k)$. We set $r = \alpha = 16$ and attach adapters to all attention
and MLP projections in both the language and vision towers, with no dropout and
no bias terms. Only $A$ and $B$ receive gradients, giving 28.9M trainable
parameters, or 1.45\% of the 2B-parameter backbone.

The objective is the standard autoregressive cross-entropy restricted to answer
tokens, so that the model is not rewarded for reproducing the prompt:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}
\sum_{t \in \mathcal{Y}} \log p_\theta\!\left(y_t \mid y_{<t},\, \mathbf{v},\, q\right),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{v}$ denotes the visual token sequence, $q$ the question, and
$\mathcal{Y}$ the index set of answer tokens.

\subsection{Resolution and the Visual Token Budget}
Qwen2-VL encodes images at native aspect ratio into patches of side $p = 14$,
merging $m \times m$ neighbourhoods ($m = 2$) before the language decoder. The
visual token count for an image of height $H$ and width $W$ is therefore
approximately

\begin{equation}
T_v \;\approx\; \frac{1}{m^{2}}
\left\lfloor \frac{H}{p} \right\rfloor
\left\lfloor \frac{W}{p} \right\rfloor .
\label{eq:tokens}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:tokens} makes the resolution trade-off explicit and
quantitative. Resizing a fundus photograph so that its longer side is 512 pixels
yields $T_v \approx 216$ tokens; 768 pixels yields 486; 1024 pixels yields 888.
Because clinically decisive lesions differ by orders of magnitude in scale---a
microaneurysm subtends only a few pixels of the original $4288$-pixel image,
whereas a cotton-wool spot subtends hundreds---the choice of $T_v$ determines
which findings survive downsampling at all. We treat resolution as a
first-class hyperparameter and ablate it in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Fitting Training into 6\,GB}
Table~\ref{tab:config} lists the configuration. Memory is controlled by four
compounding measures: 4-bit backbone quantisation, Unsloth gradient
checkpointing~\cite{unsloth2024}, a per-device batch size of one with gradient
accumulation of eight (preserving an effective batch of eight), and bfloat16
activations throughout---no tensor is materialised in fp32. Optimiser state is
held in 8-bit AdamW, which matters because adapter gradients, not frozen weights,
dominate the optimiser footprint once the backbone is quantised.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}
All evaluation is performed on the 103 held-out images, which are disjoint from
training by construction. Decoding is greedy throughout, so reported figures are
deterministic and reproducible. For each test image the model is queried
independently for a severity grade and for a clinical rationale; an integer grade
is recovered from the free-text response by pattern matching, and we report the
number of unparseable responses as a diagnostic. No response failed to parse in
any reported run.

\subsection{Metrics}
Grading is assessed by exact-match accuracy and by quadratic weighted
kappa~\cite{cohen1968weighted}, the conventional measure for ordinal DR grading:

\begin{equation}
\kappa = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij} O_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij} E_{ij}},
\qquad
w_{ij} = \frac{(i-j)^{2}}{(N-1)^{2}},
\label{eq:qwk}
\end{equation}

with $O$ the observed confusion matrix, $E$ its expectation under independent
marginals and $N = 5$. The quadratic weighting is the reason $\kappa$ is
preferable to accuracy here: confusing absent with proliferative disease is
penalised sixteen times more heavily than an adjacent-grade error, which matches
the clinical asymmetry of the underlying decision. We interpret magnitudes using
the conventional agreement bands of Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}.

Explanation quality is measured by ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge} for lexical
overlap and BERTScore~\cite{zhang2020bertscore} for semantic similarity against
the reference rationale. We additionally report mean per-image latency and
marginal monetary cost.

\subsection{Baselines and Ablation}
We compare the adapted model against the unmodified Qwen2-VL-2B backbone
evaluated zero-shot under an identical prompt and decoding configuration,
isolating the effect of adaptation. To separate the contribution of resolution
from that of adaptation, we additionally train an otherwise identical model at
512-pixel input. An API baseline is supported by our evaluation harness but was
not executed, as no commercial key was available; we state this explicitly rather
than omit the comparison silently.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Effect of Adaptation}
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the principal comparison. Adaptation raises
exact-match accuracy from 31.1\% to 66.0\% and quadratic weighted kappa from
0.000 to 0.763. The kappa figure is the more informative of the two. A value
indistinguishable from zero indicates that the zero-shot model's grade
predictions carry no ordinal information about the true severity: inspection of
its outputs shows verbose, generic descriptions that gravitate toward the
majority ``moderate'' category, so its 31.1\% accuracy reflects the base rate of
that class rather than any discriminative capability. The adapted model instead
attains 0.763, placing it in the upper part of the ``substantial agreement''
band~\cite{landis1977measurement}.

Explanation quality improves concurrently: ROUGE-L rises from 0.081 to 0.253 and
BERTScore from 0.000 to 0.309. Mean latency falls from 10.6 to 5.6 seconds per
image, a 47\% reduction, because the adapted model terminates at a structured
answer whereas the baseline continues to the token limit.

\subsection{Resolution Ablation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} isolates the effect of input resolution with all other
factors held fixed. Increasing the longer side from 512 to 768 pixels---raising
$T_v$ from approximately 216 to 486 tokens by
Equation~\eqref{eq:tokens}---improves accuracy by 11.6 points (54.4\% to 66.0\%)
and kappa by 0.14 (0.622 to 0.763). Explanation metrics improve more modestly,
which is consistent with the interpretation that resolution primarily affects
what the vision encoder can resolve rather than how the decoder verbalises it.

Two aspects of this result deserve emphasis. First, the gain is large relative to
what is typically obtained by tuning epochs or learning rate on a corpus of this
size, which suggests that resolution was the binding constraint rather than
optimisation. Second, the cost is negligible: peak memory was 3.52\,GB at both
settings, because gradient checkpointing recomputes activations rather than
retaining them, so the additional 270 visual tokens inflate compute and latency
but not the memory ceiling. Practitioners operating under a fixed VRAM budget
should therefore treat resolution as the first hyperparameter to spend on, not
the last.

\subsection{Error Structure}
Aggregate metrics conceal the behaviour that matters clinically, so we examine the
confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}) and the per-class breakdown
(Table~\ref{tab:perclass}) directly. Two observations follow, the second of which
qualifies the headline result substantially.

First, the ordinal structure of the errors is favourable. Of 103 test images,
66.0\% are graded exactly, a further 18.4\% fall within one grade, and 15.5\% err
by two grades or more; 84.5\% therefore land within one severity step of the
reference. This concentration near the diagonal is what
Equation~\eqref{eq:qwk} rewards and explains why $\kappa$ reaches 0.763 despite a
much lower exact-match rate.

Second, and more importantly, the model exhibits pronounced
\emph{majority-class collapse}. The predicted-label distribution is
$(45, 0, 42, 9, 7)$ for grades 0 through 4: the model assigns grade~0 or grade~2
to 84\% of all images, and it \emph{never once predicts grade~1} across the entire
test set. Per-class recall consequently ranges from 97.1\% on grade~0 to 0.0\% on
grade~1, with severe (31.6\%) and proliferative (30.8\%) disease also poorly
recovered---each predominantly misassigned to grade~2. The aggregate accuracy of
66.0\% is thus carried by the two majority classes, which together constitute 64\%
of the test partition. We regard this, rather than the headline accuracy, as the
principal finding of the error analysis, and we examine its causes in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
Contextualising these figures requires care about what is being measured. The
strongest published results on the IDRiD grading benchmark report
\emph{joint} accuracy, requiring the retinopathy grade and the macular-oedema
grade to be simultaneously correct; CANet~\cite{li2019canet} attains 65.1\% under
that definition and the leading challenge entry 63.1\%~\cite{porwal2020challenge}.
Our 66.0\% is a retinopathy-only figure and is therefore \emph{not} directly
comparable: the joint criterion is strictly harder, and specialised
discriminative classifiers evaluated on retinopathy alone---particularly those
trained on corpora one to two orders of magnitude larger---remain ahead of the
system reported here. We claim only that a 2B-parameter generative model adapted
on 413 images reaches the same broad performance regime as purpose-built
classifiers on this corpus, while additionally producing an auditable rationale
and running offline on commodity hardware.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Two Causes of Minority-Class Collapse}
The failure pattern in Table~\ref{tab:perclass} has two distinct causes, and
separating them matters because they demand different remedies.

The first is \emph{data scarcity acting through an imbalanced objective}. Grade~1
is represented by 20 training images out of 413 (Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}). Under a
token-level cross-entropy objective (Equation~\eqref{eq:loss}) with no class
reweighting, the loss is minimised almost as effectively by never emitting the
token sequence for ``mild'' as by learning its visual signature, since the
expected penalty for abandoning a class of prior probability $0.048$ is small.
The model duly learns this degenerate solution: grade~1 is predicted zero times.
This is a textbook consequence of optimising an unweighted likelihood on a skewed
corpus, and it is not remedied by higher resolution---the 512-pixel model failed
on grade~1 identically. Class-balanced sampling, loss reweighting, or explicit
oversampling of rare grades are the appropriate interventions, and we did not
apply them.

The second cause is \emph{supervision that does not encode the decision
boundary}, and it explains the specific confusion of grades~3 and~4 with grade~2.
Lesion indicators for grading-only images derive from grade-conditioned priors,
and those priors assign severe and proliferative disease near-identical lesion
vectors, because both present microaneurysms, haemorrhages and exudates. The
feature that clinically separates them---retinal neovascularisation---is absent
from the indicator vector entirely. The training rationales for the two classes
are therefore close to interchangeable, and the model is asked to discriminate a
boundary its supervision never represents. This is a limitation of dataset
construction rather than of the backbone, and it admits a concrete remedy:
propagating the pixel-level neovascularisation evidence available in the IDRiD
segmentation subset into the indicator vector, at the cost of restricting that
evidence to the 81 images for which masks exist.

We emphasise that neither cause is exposed by aggregate accuracy or by $\kappa$.
A system reporting 66.0\% accuracy and ``substantial'' ordinal agreement is, on
inspection, one that cannot detect mild disease at all---precisely the stage at
which screening intervention is most valuable. This dissociation between headline
metrics and clinical utility is, in our view, the most transferable lesson of the
present study.

\subsection{The Limits of These Explanations}
A second consequence of label-conditioned generation warrants explicit
statement, because it bounds the claims this paper can support. Our reference
rationales are derived from expert annotations, so they are factually consistent
with ground truth; but they are not evidence that the model's internal
computation follows the reasoning it articulates. A fluent, correct-sounding
rationale accompanying a correct grade does not establish that the rationale
caused the grade. This is the failure mode that Ghassemi
\textit{et al.}~\cite{ghassemi2021false} identify in medical explainability
generally, and our architecture does not escape it. A related weakness is
mechanical: grade and rationale are obtained from independent queries, so the two
can disagree---we observed instances in which a correct grade accompanied a
rationale describing a different severity. Joint decoding of a single structured
report would remove this inconsistency and is the most immediate improvement we
can identify.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
Subject to those caveats, the engineering result is clear. Adaptation of an
explainable DR grader required approximately 90 minutes and 3.52\,GB of VRAM,
which is within the envelope of consumer hardware widely available to individual
researchers, teaching hospitals and screening programmes in resource-constrained
settings. Inference is fully local, incurring no per-image cost and no
transmission of patient imagery to third-party infrastructure---a property with
governance value independent of accuracy. The finding that resolution dominates
is additionally actionable: it identifies the cheapest available lever for
practitioners who cannot enlarge their corpus.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Our evidence base is a single-centre corpus of 516 images captured on one camera
system; generalisation across devices, populations and image quality regimes is
untested and should not be assumed. The test partition contains only five
grade~1 images, so per-class estimates for mild disease carry wide and unreported
uncertainty. We report a single training run rather than a distribution over
seeds, so the ablation in Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} should be read as a strong
indication rather than a variance-controlled measurement. Finally, no clinician
assessed the faithfulness or usefulness of the generated rationales; automatic
overlap metrics such as ROUGE-L and BERTScore measure similarity to a reference
text, which is a weak proxy for clinical adequacy.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that explainable diabetic retinopathy grading can be adapted
end-to-end within the memory budget of a single 6\,GB consumer GPU. Combining a
compact vision-language backbone, a label-grounded question-answering corpus and
4-bit low-rank adaptation, the resulting system raises exact-match accuracy from
31.1\% to 66.0\% and quadratic weighted kappa from 0.000 to 0.763 on the
canonical IDRiD test split, while emitting a structured clinical rationale and
operating offline at no marginal cost. Our ablation identifies input resolution
as the dominant factor for this task, contributing 11.6 accuracy points at
unchanged peak memory---a result we expect to transfer to other tasks whose
decisive image features are small relative to the frame.

We are deliberate about what has not been shown, because the aggregate figures
above are not the whole result. Per-class analysis reveals that the system
collapses onto the majority grades: it never predicts mild retinopathy and
recovers severe and proliferative disease in fewer than a third of cases. A model
that cannot detect early disease is not a screening instrument, whatever its
kappa. We trace this to two separable causes---an unweighted objective over a
corpus in which mild disease is 4.8\% of training images, and lesion indicators
that do not encode the neovascularisation boundary---and note that neither is
visible in accuracy or $\kappa$ alone. Its rationales, similarly, are consistent
with ground truth but not demonstrated to be faithful to its own computation, and
its evidence base is a single-centre corpus of 516 images.

The natural continuations follow from these limits rather than from the results:
class-balanced or reweighted training to recover the minority grades,
propagation of pixel-level neovascularisation evidence into supervision, joint
decoding of grade and rationale so the two cannot diverge, extension across
multiple public corpora to test cross-device generalisation, and---indispensably
before any claim of clinical utility---a reader study in which ophthalmologists
assess whether the generated rationales are faithful and actionable. We release
the dataset, adapter weights and full training and evaluation code to make each
of these steps directly reproducible.\footnote{Code:
\url{https://github.com/vermayuvraj/idrid-vqa}. Model:
\url{https://huggingface.co/vermayuvraj/idrid-qwen2vl-2b-qlora}. Dataset:
\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/vermayuvraj/idrid-vqa}.}

\section*{Ethics Statement}
This work is a research prototype and is not a medical device. Its outputs must
not be used for diagnosis, triage or treatment decisions. The study uses the
publicly available IDRiD dataset~\cite{porwal2018idrid} under its licence and
involves no new patient recruitment and no personally identifying information.
Reference rationales are model-generated and may contain clinical errors despite
being conditioned on expert labels. We note that a system which produces fluent
clinical language can invite unwarranted confidence, and that this risk is
heightened rather than reduced when the language is well-formed; deployment would
require prospective validation and human oversight.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}
All results derive from a single publicly available dataset and a released
codebase. Decoding is greedy and seeds are fixed, so reported metrics are
